\lessontitle{03}{Selection}

\begin{frame}{Population and question}

Ten candidates have fitness values ordered from $-5$ to $4$. Selection returns ten copies of these same candidates. Who reproduces, who disappears, and how many copies does the champion get?

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proportional probability}

For nonnegative weights $w_i$, $p_i=w_i/\sum_j w_j$ and expected copies are $Np_i$. Fitness includes negative values, so a floor is added. That floor changes $p_i$ and therefore selection pressure.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rank and scale}

Rank selection assigns weights by position, not fitness distance. A monotone fitness transformation preserves order and rank probabilities. Even so, one sample can lose the champion.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Elitism and SUS}

Elitism copies the champion unchanged before filling other slots; with fixed fitness it preserves the best. Stochastic universal sampling (SUS) uses equally spaced pointers: the same expected counts as roulette with less sampling variation.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Tournament as a control}

A size-$k$ tournament with replacement chooses the best of $k$ draws. As $k$ grows, expected champion copies rise and diversity tends to fall. $k=3$ is a starting configuration, not a universal answer.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measure before choosing}

Track champion copies, lost individuals, mean fitness, and gene spread. Across 13 configurations in the extensive sequence, pressure correlates $+0.95$ with mean fitness and $-0.92$ with spread. These are case observations, not laws.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Apply to Planta Física}

Selection cannot repair infeasible plans: the evaluator or documented repair must do so. To compare $k$, hold the 48-decision evaluator, seeds, and evaluation budget fixed.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exit ticket}

Choose an initial selector and justify its pressure. State one diversity-loss risk and the metric to watch. Archive the best plan seen outside the population if elitism is not guaranteed.

\end{frame}
